Data Cleaning: A Necessity or a Failure of System Design?

Investigating preventable data quality issues in databases, spreadsheets and controlled data collection systems.

25 November 2026 · Real-world case study · In progress
ContentsIntroductionScopePrevention at the sourceLimits of validationExperimental approachReferences

Introduction

Data cleaning is commonly taught as a fundamental stage of data analysis. Missing values, duplicate records, invalid formats and inconsistent categories are familiar examples. Yet many of the systems that produce these records already support mechanisms for preventing such errors.

Relational databases offer integrity constraints. Spreadsheets support data validation, and applications can enforce business rules before accepting input. These capabilities raise a practical engineering question: why should an organisation repeatedly correct predictable errors if it can prevent them during the initial design of its information systems?

Businesses were already using databases and statistical analysis in the 1990s. Data quality was an established concern before modern AI. This article examines the relationship between system design and recurring cleaning effort, rather than questioning the value of data analysis itself.

Scope of the investigation

The focus is on controllable sources: relational databases, Excel and other spreadsheets, electronic forms, applications, APIs and structured imports. The investigation concerns errors for which a reliable validation rule can be specified in advance. It does not assume that all data quality problems can be prevented.

Research question: To what extent can preventive controls introduced during system design reduce downstream data cleaning without compromising completeness or analytical accuracy?

Prevention at the source

Consider an employee management system with a defined monthly salary range. If the organisation has established that a particular category of employees earns between €1,500 and €9,999, an accidental entry of €30,000 can be rejected before storage.

monthly_salary DECIMAL(10,2) NOT NULL
CHECK (monthly_salary BETWEEN 1500 AND 9999)

The range is hypothetical, not a universal salary rule. A real implementation must account for employment categories, exceptions and changes in policy. In Excel, a decimal data validation rule with a Stop alert can enforce the same range during ordinary direct entry.

Recurring issuePreventive mechanism
Duplicate identifiersDatabase uniqueness constraints
Missing mandatory fieldsRequired form inputs and NOT NULL
Inconsistent categoriesControlled spreadsheet dropdowns or reference tables
Invalid numerical rangesBusiness rules and CHECK constraints
Invalid structured importsSchema validation before acceptance

These controls should not merely reject errors silently. They should provide feedback and a way to correct or quarantine problematic records. Repeated downstream correction may otherwise indicate that a known rule has not been enforced at the appropriate boundary.

Limits of validation

A valid value is not necessarily a truthful one. A salary of €3,500 may satisfy the range while the actual salary is €3,000. Excel validation can also be bypassed by certain copying or importing operations. Uniqueness constraints cannot identify every duplicate real-world entity. Controls must therefore be designed around actual input paths and supported by appropriate verification.

Sensor noise, genuinely unavailable information and uncontrolled external sources are outside the primary scope. Their existence does not remove the opportunity to prevent predictable errors in systems that organisations do control.

Real-world case study: Online Retail

The main empirical study uses the original Online Retail workbook published by Daqing Chen through the UCI Machine Learning Repository (2015). It contains 541,909 transaction-line observations from a UK online retailer between 1 December 2010 and 9 December 2011, with eight attributes. The dataset is licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Audit design

We will examine the original workbook without injecting errors or changing observations. The audit counts missing fields, exact repeated rows, negative and zero prices, negative quantities and cancellation invoices. Candidate validation rules are evaluated separately from descriptive observations.

Observed conditionPotential design controlInterpretation
Missing core transaction fieldsMandatory fields and schema checksDepends on business requirements
Missing customer identifierConditional requirement for identified customersAnonymous sales may be legitimate
Exact repeated rowsStable transaction-line identifier and idempotent writesNot automatically duplicate transactions
Negative unit priceConditional price validationAccounting adjustments may be valid
Negative quantity or cancellationExplicit transaction type and conditional rulesReturns are not inherently errors

Evaluation and limitations

We will replay the original observations through proposed validation policies and report flagged rows, overlap and review requirements. The workbook alone cannot establish the retailer's original validation controls, ground truth, or whether an anomaly arose during collection, export or transformation. Consequently, flag rates are not proven error-prevention rates. Excel input validation, SQL constraints and application checks will be compared as proposed controls, not as claims about the retailer's existing system.

The real-data audit script is available, but the original workbook could not be downloaded in the execution environment. Results are pending. The earlier synthetic pilot is excluded from the main empirical evidence.
