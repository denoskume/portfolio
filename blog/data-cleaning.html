Data Cleaning: A Necessity or a Failure of System Design?

What 541,909 real retail transaction lines reveal about preventable data quality problems.

25 November 2026 · Case study
ContentsIntroductionData and methodProduct descriptionsRepeated invoice linesMissing informationQuantity and priceDesign recommendationsConclusionReferences

Introduction

Data cleaning is often described as an essential first step in data analytics. But when the data originate from a database, a spreadsheet or another controlled information system, an important question arises: which of the problems encountered by analysts could have been avoided when the system was designed?

Database constraints, reference tables, spreadsheet validation and application-level business rules have existed for decades. Rather than demonstrating them on an invented dataset, this article examines actual retail records to identify concrete opportunities for preventive design. The objective is not to remove every unusual record. It is to identify what a system could have represented or validated more effectively at the source.

The data and method

The Online Retail dataset, published by Daqing Chen through UCI [1], records transactions from a UK online retailer between December 2010 and December 2011. The original Excel workbook was opened and audited with pandas; no records were deleted, corrected or artificially generated.

The audit examines relationships between fields as well as individual values. This is essential: missing descriptions, zero prices and missing customer identifiers may be related rather than three independent problems.

1. Product descriptions: why repeat reference data?

Grouping the non-missing descriptions by StockCode shows that 650 distinct item codes have more than one recorded description. A striking example is code 85123A.

StockCodeDescription in workbookOccurrences
85123AWHITE HANGING HEART T-LIGHT HOLDER2,302
85123ACREAM HANGING HEART T-LIGHT HOLDER9
85123A?1
85123Awrongly marked carton 228041

The question mark and carton comment are particularly revealing: the same exported field contains both product naming and operational information. Replacing every description with the most common value could destroy useful information or incorrectly relabel a product variant.

A preventive design would maintain an approved product catalogue keyed by StockCode, record catalogue changes when needed, and keep operational comments in a separate field. Transactions would reference the catalogue instead of requiring staff to repeatedly type a product name.

In SQL, a foreign key can ensure that the product code exists in the catalogue. In Excel, a controlled list and a lookup formula can retrieve the approved description. These mechanisms reduce inconsistent manual entry, provided catalogue ownership and versioning are also defined.

2. Repeated invoice lines: a question of identity

The workbook contains 5,268 exact repeated rows beyond the first occurrence, approximately 0.97% of all lines. Consider invoice 536409, where the following observed record appears more than once.

InvoiceNoStockCodeDescriptionQuantityUnitPriceInvoiceDateCustomerID
53640921866UNION JACK FLAG LUGGAGE TAG1£1.252010-12-01 11:4517908
53640921866UNION JACK FLAG LUGGAGE TAG1£1.252010-12-01 11:4517908

Both rows are identical across all eight attributes, including United Kingdom as the country. But are they two separate purchased items or a duplicated recording? The workbook cannot answer because it does not contain a distinct transaction-line identifier.

Preventing this ambiguity requires a stable identifier assigned when each line is created, not an arbitrary uniqueness rule on InvoiceNo or on all eight exported fields. The database can enforce uniqueness on that identifier, and the application can reuse it when retrying a submission. In Excel, a protected identifier column and controlled import process can serve a similar role, although spreadsheet uniqueness is less robust than database enforcement.

Deleting the 5,268 repeats automatically would assume that all of them represent invalid events. The observed data do not justify that assumption.

3. Missing values: examine the relationships first

All 1,454 lines with missing descriptions also have missing CustomerID and a zero UnitPrice. The same combination is visible in invoice 536414 and several other records:

This pattern suggests that these lines may represent a particular operational process rather than unrelated omissions. The event type is not provided, so the hypothesis cannot be confirmed. Requiring every customer identifier or description indiscriminately could suppress legitimate records.

A system designed around explicitly declared transaction types could specify which attributes are mandatory for each process. Where a description is derived from the product catalogue, it need not be manually entered. Where customer identification is required, a validated customer reference can enforce that rule. Missing information should be distinguished from information that is not applicable.

4. Quantity and price: business rules, not arbitrary thresholds

The workbook contains 10,624 lines with negative quantities. UCI documents a C prefix on InvoiceNo as a cancellation [1]. All 9,288 cancellation lines have negative quantities. A universal positive-quantity constraint would therefore flag every cancellation line.

The price field offers another example. Exactly two records have negative UnitPrice, and both are labelled “Adjust bad debt”.

InvoiceNoStockCodeDescriptionQuantityUnitPrice
A563186BAdjust bad debt1−£11,062.06

There are also 2,515 zero-price lines. These facts do not establish that the records are correct, but they demonstrate that unusual numerical values can belong to different business processes. Instead of a universal price restriction, a proposed design would require a declared event type and apply the appropriate authorised policy.

From observed problems to preventive system design

The audit supports specific architectural recommendations, rather than a generic cleaning procedure.

Observed evidenceProposed upstream mechanismRemaining question
650 codes with multiple descriptionsProduct reference table; separate notes; versioningWhich descriptions are approved?
5,268 exact repeated rowsStable line identifier and idempotent writesWhich repeats are genuine duplicate events?
1,454 missing descriptions with zero prices and no customer IDExplicit event type and conditional required fieldsWhich operational process generated them?
9,288 negative-quantity cancellationsCancellation-specific validationWhat are the rules for the other negative quantities?
2 negative-price adjustment linesAuthorised accounting-adjustment workflowWere these adjustments correct?

PostgreSQL provides primary keys, foreign keys and CHECK constraints [2]. Excel provides validation lists and custom formulas [3]. Applications and APIs can enforce equivalent rules before accepting records. The specific choice depends on how the organisation controls entry, bulk imports and storage. Validation must include legitimate exceptions and a correction or review path.

The proposed controls have not been deployed against the retailer's original system. The audit measures observable conditions, not verified error counts or actual prevention rates. A controlled intervention would require business policies, source-system logs and confirmation from the process owners.

Conclusion

Looking at real transaction records changes the nature of the data cleaning discussion. The product code 85123A raises a reference-data design question. Invoice 536409 raises an event-identity question. The 1,454 lines with jointly missing information and zero prices raise a question about how operational events are represented. Negative cancellation quantities show why business semantics must precede numerical validation.

These are not simply opportunities to apply pandas cleaning functions. They are opportunities to improve how information is collected, represented and governed. When a predictable problem can be prevented at the source, repeated downstream correction should not be the default response. The analyst's role includes identifying such problems and feeding evidence back to the people responsible for system design.

Not all cleaning can be eliminated. But data quality should be treated as a design requirement, not exclusively as a task performed after collection.
